The model that remains unchanged

The main gains come from operating and validating the existing framework correctly.

The current AIT research model blends 75% counts-only risk with 25% counts-and-state risk, then applies the retained calibration. The state branch uses Bayesian evidence and forward Hidden Markov Model (HMM) state estimates. It forecasts the successful-root outcome at 1, 2, 5, 10, 15 and 30 minutes.

Existing experts; new automatic routing

Why: An offline expert can only handle an outage correctly if the runtime selects it and supplies the right history.

How: The service chooses among the seven nonempty subsets of AMiner, Suricata and Wazuh. Forecast weights, calibration and thresholds stay fixed.

Verified result: All 56 fold-specific experts reproduce 2,007,012 retained forecast values with zero error. All 65 files checked inside the frozen package retain their original checksums.

Keep fitting, validation and testing distinct

Why: Fitting on the evaluated campaign or using its future outcomes during inference would make reported results misleading.

How: Each original fold uses six fitting campaigns, one validation campaign and one test campaign. Prefix tests compare predictions before later data is supplied; test truth/onset fields are removed or poisoned during inference checks.

Verified result: The eight-fold conformance check passes: earlier predictions remain causal, test labels do not change inference, and probability vectors stay bounded and coherent across horizons.

Boundary: These campaigns were inspected during development. The exercise is a reconstruction and functional audit, not a fresh independent performance study.

Fewer unnecessary alarms

The quieter policy is available explicitly; original responsiveness is the default.

Confirmation and bounded episode handling

Why: Brief threshold crossings and repeated notifications can increase operator burden. Suppression, however, can also remove useful short signals.

How: The confirmed profile requires two consecutive above-threshold minute buckets. It uses two below-clear-threshold buckets, a clear ratio of 0.8, a 120-second maximum hold below the warning threshold, and a 300-second notification cooldown. Context changes and long gaps reset episode state.

Verified result: At 30 minutes with all sensors, false episodes fall from 5 to 2 (60% fewer); notifications fall from 14 to 8 (42.9% fewer). The same five campaigns receive timely notifications.

Separate warning visibility from notification traffic

Why: A cooldown bug could hide a confirmed warning until another outbound notification was permitted.

How: Warning state, episode starts and notification emission are tracked independently. Cooldown limits outbound traffic while the confirmed episode remains visible. A pending notification can be emitted if high risk persists until cooldown expires.

Verified result: An independent policy oracle reproduces all 112 campaign/sensor/horizon cases and 14 aggregates. Both episode and notification metrics are retained.

Why the quieter profile is not the default

First timely notification is later in four of the five warned campaigns; fox loses 10 minutes of lead. At 15 minutes, coverage falls from 4/8 to 3/8. The higher aggregate mean lead does not mean every warning became earlier. The responsive default exactly preserves original warning/notification traces in all 112 fixed-availability cases.

Both profiles use the same forecast probabilities. The full-sensor 30-minute mean Brier remains 0.001460568. No probability-accuracy gain is attributed to alarm suppression.

Stronger operation with missing sensors

An unavailable sensor is different from a healthy sensor reporting zero alerts.

Source-health routing and honest abstention

Why: Zero-filling a missing source can falsely present an outage as evidence of quiet traffic. A stale sensor can also contaminate trailing features.

How: Heartbeats describe source health independently of alert counts. A missing heartbeat or one older than 90 seconds marks a sensor unavailable. Its counts are ignored; the matching existing expert is selected. If no sensors remain, risk and action are withheld.

Verified result: All seven nonempty sensor subsets, total outage, stale/missing heartbeats and recovery pass controlled operation checks. Deliberately poisoned unavailable-sensor counts do not change forecasts.

Boundary: Health must represent the complete collection path. A sensor claiming to be healthy can still suffer silent partial loss that this interface cannot infer.

Preserve usable history; reset broken history

Why: Fallback needs valid past observations. Reusing stale history after an outage or gap can create an incorrect temporal state.

How: All experts whose required sensors are healthy continue to advance. On gaps or re-entry after an outage, affected histories restart. Each stream retains at most 60 minutes of feature history.

Verified result: Stream results match independently rebuilt batch prefixes, including transitions and skipped buckets. Cold recovery is explicit rather than silently filled with invented observations.

Live timing and response measurement

A running local service was exercised; deployment effectiveness is still outstanding.

Introduce a streaming shadow-mode API

Why: Offline scores alone do not show whether observations can be processed through an actual service, or whether its behavior remains correct during outages.

How: The /api/live service accepts normalized finalized one-minute count buckets and source heartbeats. Replay mode permits archived timestamps; live mode requires buckets finalized within the previous 120 seconds, with one second of clock tolerance.

Verified result: A real local HTTP process handled 180 accelerated fox observations, all sensor subsets, total outage, recovery and a current-time live smoke observation. Probabilities and modeled actions were compared with batch results.

Boundary: No production AMiner, Suricata or Wazuh feed was connected. Collector adapters and end-to-end deployment behavior were not measured.

Make warning and response timing auditable

Why: Without observation, receipt and notification timestamps, server speed can be mistaken for end-to-end warning latency.

How: Notification payloads retain observation time, server receipt time, stream mode, warning profile and decision age. Live bucket-to-notification age is recorded when meaningful; historical replay is not assigned a misleading wall-clock age.

Verified result: The storage and timing paths are exercised by API tests and the HTTP benchmark. The API exposes /status for model readiness and /report for saved results.

Persist response outcomes without claiming prevention

Why: An acknowledgment or completed handler does not establish that a threat was stopped, but these operations must be measurable before a real trial.

How: SQLite stores emitted, acknowledged, completed or failed receipt state. Consumers post response receipts; server arrival timestamps produce acknowledgment, completion and resolution timing.

Verified result: The final benchmark records 5/5 acknowledgments and 5 no-op completions. Median acknowledgment is 8.99 ms and median no-op completion is 16.55 ms after emission.

Boundary: Only five test notification samples are present in this final run. No defensive actuator was connected and no causal reduction in attack success was measured.

Status of the original live-environment objective

Partially fulfilled. Service behavior, local handling latency and response-recording infrastructure are implemented. Real deployment latency and response effectiveness still require a controlled trial with actual source feeds and verified endpoint outcomes.

Reliable retries, failures and persistence

These fixes address concrete operational defects rather than improving forecast accuracy.

1. Idempotent observation retries

Why: A lost HTTP reply can make a caller retry an already processed bucket, advancing history twice or creating duplicate notifications.

How: The most recently committed normalized observation has a canonical fingerprint and cached response. An identical retry returns the same result and notification ID. Different evidence at that timestamp returns 409; older buckets are rejected.

Verified result: The stress flows check 48 exact retries and 16 conflicts. Concurrent identical-retry tests verify one commit.

Boundary: The cache is process-local and covers only the latest observation; it is not durable restart recovery.

2. Atomic state rollback after failure

Why: Forecast or database failure could leave temporal state advanced even though the caller received an error.

How: Before mutation, the service checkpoints expert histories and warning state. Inference or notification-storage exceptions restore both and return 503 so the same bucket can be retried.

Verified result: 3 real-model persistence faults recover in stress testing; additional tests inject inference failures and verify unchanged state before retry.

3. Immutable receipt timing and outcomes

Why: Repeated or contradictory receipts could overwrite the first timing or note, or change a completed/failed result.

How: Duplicate receipts preserve the first acknowledgment and note. Terminal states reject incompatible changes. Failed outcomes now record resolution time; backward-clock receipts are rejected instead of recording negative latency.

Verified result: Tests cover duplicate acknowledgment and terminal receipts, incompatible transitions, failed resolution timing and a backward clock.

4. Close database connections on all paths

Why: An unclosed connection is a resource leak that can accumulate over repeated operations or failed transactions.

How: Database context managers commit or roll back transactions and close connections in a finally block, including exception paths.

Verified result: Backend checks cover database lifecycle behavior alongside persistence faults. This fixes a connection-resource leak; it is not evidence that a data-exfiltration incident occurred.

Input and local-service safeguards

Reject malformed or excessive work before it can corrupt state or mislead callers.

5. Validate normalized evidence and work limits

Why: Non-finite values, overflowing count totals, invalid timestamps and unbounded generation requests can crash processing, poison histories or waste resources.

How: Typed schemas reject extra fields, invalid sensor/detector names, nonnumeric/negative/non-finite counts and overflowing totals. Counts are capped at 4,096 entries. Live timestamps and minute-grid checks agree with the stream model. Legacy batches are bounded to 500 events; synthetic generation to 5,000 steps with rates in [0,1].

Verified result: Malformed, forged-label/action, NaN/Infinity, extreme-count, time-grid and work-bound tests verify rejection without advancing temporal state.

6. Protect SQL identifiers and synthetic run boundaries

Why: Parameterized values do not protect an interpolated table name. A blank run ID could also make synthetic replacement affect other runs.

How: Table identifiers are allowlisted. Synthetic writes require a nonblank run ID of at most 128 characters. Run values remain bound SQL data; event dictionaries are copied rather than modified. Empty generation removes only its own previous run.

Verified result: Tests reject untrusted identifiers, verify quoted IDs remain data, preserve other runs, and confirm caller input dictionaries are unchanged.

7. Restrict the local HTTP boundary

Why: Cross-site browser requests or remote peers should not be able to mutate this unauthenticated research service.

How: The API checks the actual loopback peer, trusted Host and declared local browser origins; spoofed forwarded headers do not establish trust. Duplicate Origin/length headers and ambiguous length encodings are rejected. Bodies are capped at 2 MiB, including chunked requests, with a ten-second receive timeout.

Verified result: Tests exercise remote peers, spoofed headers, forbidden origins, cross-site fetches, oversized/chunked/slow bodies and declared-length mismatches.

Boundary: This is a local boundary, not production authentication. Any proxy exposing it externally would need a separate deployment design.

8. Fail safely on invalid model packages and errors

Why: A file can deserialize while still missing experts, having invalid probability matrices or mismatched feature dimensions. Default errors can also echo rejected input.

How: A checksum is checked before loading the fixed trusted-local pickle. Structural validation checks all seven experts, fitting metadata, dimensions, probability normalization, calibrators and horizons. Validation responses omit rejected input values.

Verified result: Missing, corrupt, unloadable and structurally partial packages cannot report ready; non-finite/secret-like rejected inputs are exercised without reflecting their values.

Boundary: Pickle remains trusted local code. Checksums are not signatures, and a hostile local writer remains outside this protection.

Earlier-demo correctness and isolation

These changes apply to the DARPA/synthetic demonstration, separately from AIT forecast scores.

9. Independent causal historical replay

Why: Seeking to an earlier alert through a shared stateful inference service could reuse later belief state or reset another caller.

How: Each historical batch owns a fresh temporal pipeline while reusing fitted read-only models. The dashboard caches each causal per-event result and seeks locally. Earlier timestamps on an individual stream are rejected unless that caller resets it.

Verified result: Batch prefixes match separate shorter runs. Browser tests step forward and seek/reset backward without calling the state-mutating /pipeline/infer route; past recommendations stay identical.

10. Separate individual clients

Why: One shared individual-inference history allows one client or reset operation to affect another client.

How: Opaque HttpOnly, SameSite=Strict cookies identify per-client temporal contexts under /api/pipeline. Contexts expire after an hour of inactivity, with a 32-context cap. Each reset affects only its own context.

Verified result: Two-client tests verify independent histories and reset behavior.

Boundary: Cookies isolate anonymous local state; they are not authenticated user identities. Contexts are process-local.

11. Bounded history and truthful provenance

Why: Unbounded legacy history wastes memory. Recording a posterior as the prior misstates how a recommendation was reached.

How: The legacy observation history is capped at 15 and event history at 30. The previous dominant state is captured before filter update and recorded as the prior. A B0 rule-expression precedence issue is corrected so a flood signature works even when a port is empty or nonnumeric.

Verified result: Tests exercise 100 sequential events, validate history bounds and prior traces, and confirm flood-signature behavior across port representations.

12. Serialize mutable evaluation runners

Why: Concurrent legacy evaluation runs can interleave mutable shared runner state and mix results.

How: A reentrant lock serializes endpoints that operate on the shared legacy evaluation runner. Independent current-model streams and historical batches retain their own state.

Verified result: A concurrency check verifies the guarded legacy runner does not interleave.

Boundary: Serialization can reduce throughput for simultaneous legacy evaluation requests. It is a correctness choice, not a performance gain.

Trustworthy decisions and simulations

Optimal means best under the specified mathematical assumptions, not guaranteed real-world success.

13. Use unrounded expected utilities to choose actions

Why: Rounding utilities before ranking can create an artificial tie and select the wrong action.

How: The legacy Decision Network validates the supplied probability distribution and chooses the maximum using full-precision utility values. Formatting is kept separate from selection; unknown actions and invalid baselines are rejected.

Verified result: A deliberately close-utility example and 200 seeded probability distributions confirm selection agrees with direct expected-utility maximization.

14. Remove invented differences between identical actions

Why: Different trajectory rules for the baseline and hypothetical series could show a benefit even when both selected actions were identical.

How: Both series now use the same explicitly illustrative cumulative-loss rule. Equal action and belief inputs therefore produce equal trajectories and zero difference. Both bar series use one actual scale, including zero-height bars, and the UI uses model units instead of money/prevention claims.

Verified result: Tests compare every action with itself. Real-browser checks confirm identical Monitor losses and equal bar heights, and reject stale simulation replies.

Boundary: Trajectory progression and effectiveness are assumptions. Existing cached legacy RQ reports may predate this correction and are labeled separately.

15. Account for forecast age in live action costs

Why: Applying a fresh-observation action formula to an older bucket can overestimate the time left for an intervention.

How: Live costs use receipt-time age plus action delay, while keeping the endpoint at observation time + 30 minutes. Onset probability between saved horizons is linearly interpolated. Replay keeps the original observation-time formula exactly.

Verified result: Two hundred seeded coherent risk vectors are checked against an independent interpolation/loss calculation. Modeled action costs do not optimistically improve simply because the forecast gets older.

Boundary: Interpolation is a decision-model assumption, not a newly calibrated horizon or a new forecast for 30 minutes after receipt.

The current response-cost rule

disruption + 20 × [p30 − 0.8 × effect × max(0, p30 − CDF(age + action delay))]
For replay, age = 0. The service selects the lowest-cost action among Monitor, Restrict, Block and Isolate. These checks verify arithmetic under configured costs; they do not verify assumed effectiveness in a real incident.

Dashboard and evidence integrity

A correct model is not sufficient if the interface pairs it with stale data or unverified results.

16. Prevent old HTTP replies from replacing new choices

Why: Rapid changes of horizon, campaign, scenario, mode or action can cause a slower old response to arrive last and display results for the wrong controls.

How: Responses and failures are keyed to their inputs. Requests are aborted or their stale results ignored on changes. Playback resets appropriately, loading states show no invented beliefs, and what-if action selection is preserved. Errors clear on successful retrieval.

Verified result: Real-browser tests intentionally reorder horizon, legacy-mode and simulation replies; the newest controls retain the correct results. Play/pause/step/reset/jump/campaign-change operations pass without browser errors or API server failures.

17. Verify evidence before serving cached content

Why: Caching by filename alone can continue showing an old verified result after the file is changed or corrupted.

How: Research JSON/NPZ reads verify the frozen manifest or independently audited metric-input hash before content-keyed decoding. Plot downloads use their export manifest. Missing, changed, malformed or unverified evidence returns 503. The exact verified bytes are served for downloads.

Verified result: Isolated tamper tests prime the cache, change evidence, confirm rejection, restore evidence and confirm recovery. Original archives are never corrupted for this test.

Boundary: Local manifests detect drift; they are not signatures against an attacker who can replace both data and manifests.

18. Independently reconstruct the reported numbers

Why: Copying dashboard constants or calling the same scoring implementation twice would not independently establish that the reported metrics were computed correctly.

How: The audit recalculates Brier, rank/tie-aware average precision, strict warning episodes, lead, burden and aggregates from prediction archives. It reconstructs the original constrained threshold search, snapshots input hashes and rejects changes during a run.

Verified result: 25,440 score rows, 3,660 aggregate rows, 456 archives and 8,064 validation threshold selections pass.

Boundary: Threshold optimality is limited to the original candidate grid, budget and objective. This does not establish a globally optimal architecture.

Labels now distinguish archived replay from live service behavior, replace “Reset to Live” with “Reset to Observed Belief”, identify illustrative effects and model units, and remove an unqualified “optimal balance” claim. Production build and lint pass without frontend warnings.

Eleven warning candidates were evaluated in 112 fold/sensor/horizon settings using outer-validation forecasts and fixed thresholds. Selection required no validation loss in coverage or first lead, and no increase in false notifications, false episodes, negative warning minutes or total notifications. Consequences that remain explicit

Quieter alarms can lose lead or miss brief signals. Total sensor loss requires abstention. Input bounds reject excessive work. Transactional and validation safeguards have runtime costs. Streams reset on restart, and multi-worker/durable stream recovery is not implemented. Default 30-minute coverage remains 5/8, not perfect coverage.

Outstanding: fresh independent campaigns; complete source-feed adapters; dynamic-outage accuracy after cold recovery; production-load and security assessment; authenticated durable deployment; and a controlled actuator trial with verified attack outcomes. A narrow source scan found no matching key/private-key patterns, but excluded data/private folders and was not an exhaustive credential or dependency audit. Existing Python environment warnings were observed; frontend lint is clean.
